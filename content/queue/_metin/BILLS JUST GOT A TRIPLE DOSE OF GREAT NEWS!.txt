BILLS JUST GOT A TRIPLE DOSE OF GREAT NEWS!

Bills Mafia, the Buffalo Bills went into Minnesota on Monday Night Football without their starting left tackle and their starting left guard. They faced a Vikings defense that blitzes more than anybody in football. And they won 23 to 6.

But that’s not even the craziest part. Buffalo’s defense generated more pressure than Minnesota’s, while blitzing just 11.5 percent of the time. Jim Leonhard beat Brian Flores at his own game.

And that’s just the start of the good news. Because this win came with a triple dose of it: the backup offensive line held up against the toughest test in football, the defense put together one of its most dominant performances in years, and two Bills stars hit a milestone together that no teammates in NFL history had ever reached.

Buffalo is now seven and one for the first time since 1993.

Let’s go back to Monday afternoon, when the inactive list came out.

Dion Dawkins: inactive. Alec Anderson: inactive.

Both starters on the left side of the offensive line were out. That meant Tylan Grable at left tackle and Austin Corbett at left guard.

The Bills’ post announcing the inactives got more than 670,000 views. Bills fans everywhere saw it, and most of us had the same reaction: uh-oh. Bills reporter Matt Parrino called it a huge spot for Grable and Corbett. And in pregame warmups, there they were, working with the starters.

And here’s why this felt so scary. The last time Buffalo lost both starting tackles, the team still found a way to win. But this time, the defense across the field was built to attack exactly that kind of weakness.

Coming into this game, Minnesota had blitzed on 73.6 percent of opponents’ dropbacks through seven games. That’s the highest rate Next Gen Stats has ever tracked. Athlon called this game a blitz test for Buffalo’s backup line. Buffalo Rumblings said the Bills had to prove they could adapt.

Josh Allen said it himself before the game. He said this defense has no weaknesses, and that if you turn your eyes the wrong way, you’re already wrong.

So the stage was set. A backup left tackle. A backup left guard. Against the most aggressive blitz in football.

And Brian Flores did exactly what everyone expected.

He blitzed Josh Allen on 68.6 percent of his dropbacks, the most Josh has ever been blitzed in a single game in his career.

And early on, it worked. Minnesota got to Josh. Buffalo had to lean on quick throws, like one to Khalil Shakir, just to keep the chains moving. And James Cook couldn’t find any room to run.

If you were watching at home, you were probably thinking the same thing a lot of us were: here it comes.

But here’s the twist. The backups weren’t the problem.

That’s great news number one.

Bills reporter Chris Brown’s verdict after the game was simple: Grable and Corbett handled it. And Joe Brady made sure to give credit to the depth on his offensive line.

Bills Mafia, remember what Brady said the week before? He said Grable had grown so much working with offensive line coaches Pat and Austin Gunn, that he had essentially played two games already, and there hadn’t been any hesitation about what the team could do with him. Brady said that when backups like Grable go in, he doesn’t feel like he has to call a different football game. And he explained that in practice, the Bills intentionally rotate offensive line combinations, because that’s the reality of NFL games.

Well, on Monday night, that preparation paid off in the biggest way possible.

And as the line held up, Josh Allen started finding answers, like a well-timed throw to Ty Johnson.

But here’s the stat that tells the real story. It isn’t about what happened when Flores blitzed. It’s about what happened when he didn’t.

When Minnesota sent four rushers or fewer, Josh Allen went 9 of 11 for 126 yards and a touchdown.

So the blitz never broke Buffalo. And every time Flores backed off, Josh made him pay.

One analysis put it perfectly: Josh beat the blitz with patience, not heroics.

Think about how big that is, Bills Mafia. For years, the knock on Josh Allen was that he tried to do too much. Hold the ball too long, scramble, force a throw that wasn’t there. And remember what he said after the Ravens game: he admitted he was pressing in the first half, and that he had to take a breath, settle his feet, and go through his progressions.

Against the most aggressive defense in football, he did exactly that. He took what was there. He waited for Flores to blink. And he never gave Minnesota the big mistake it was hunting for.

That’s growth. And it’s exactly what Joe Brady has been preaching all season: don’t be a hero, play within the system.

And the play that summed it all up started with Josh hanging in the pocket.

He held the ball for 6.07 seconds. In the NFL, that’s an eternity.

Vikings edge rusher Andrew Van Ginkel drilled him just as he let it go. And waiting wide open in the back of the end zone was a teammate who hadn’t been thrown the ball a single time all season.

Twenty-five-yard touchdown. His first career touchdown, on his first target of the year.

That clip has nearly 450,000 views on the NFL’s account. And think about what it means. On a night designed to rattle him, Josh found a player who hadn’t been targeted all year. That’s the kind of throw you only make when you trust your line to give you time.

And along the way, Josh tied Peyton Manning’s touchdown total through ten seasons. That came just eight days after he broke Jim Kelly’s franchise record for total touchdown passes against the Ravens.

So the backups held up, and Josh stayed patient. But the Vikings were still in the game. And that’s when Joe Brady made the decision that changed everything.

He went to the ground. Against the most blitz-happy defense in football.

Brady handed James Cook the ball 31 times. That’s the most carries by any player in any NFL game this season.

And the message was simple: if you want to blitz us, you’re going to have to tackle James Cook first. Over and over and over again.

Remember, Cook struggled early. He couldn’t find room. But he kept his legs churning, and Brady kept feeding him.

One drive went 15 plays and ate up 8 minutes and 32 seconds of clock. By the end of the night, Buffalo had held the ball for 42 minutes and 32 seconds. That’s more than two of every three minutes in the game.

The Bills ran 79 offensive plays. The Vikings ran 46. That’s 33 more snaps for Buffalo.

And Cook finished the job himself, scoring the touchdown that made it a two-score game. That’s seven straight games with a touchdown for James Cook.

Yardbarker’s headline said it best: James Cook carried the ball, and the Bills, to victory. A to Z Sports put both Brady and Cook on its list of the game’s winners.

Here’s why that strategy was so brilliant. A blitz is a gamble. You’re betting that your extra rushers get home before the ball comes out. Brady refused to take that bet. He kept handing it off, kept the clock running, and kept Minnesota’s defense on the field until it had nothing left.

And look what that did to the other side. Every minute Buffalo had the ball was a minute Justin Jefferson spent on the bench. Minnesota’s offense ran just 46 plays all night. You can’t come back when you never get the ball.

And remember what Joe Brady said earlier this season about James Cook? After the Raiders game, when Cook had a costly fumble late, someone asked Brady what he was going to do with him. Brady said: I’m going to hand him the football. He said he wouldn’t be standing where he is without James Cook, and that when Cook holds the ball, he holds the whole organization.

Thirty-one carries on Monday night. Brady meant every word.

And now, great news number two: the milestone.

Somewhere in those 31 carries, James Cook passed 5,000 career rushing yards.

And so did Josh Allen. A quarterback. Remember, Josh came into the game needing just nine rushing yards to become only the fifth quarterback in NFL history with 5,000 career rushing yards, joining Lamar Jackson, Michael Vick, Cam Newton, and Russell Wilson. And he did it in the same building where he hurdled Anthony Barr as a rookie, the moment he said he first felt like he belonged in the NFL.

And according to WGRZ, Josh Allen and James Cook are the first teammates in NFL history to both reach 5,000 career rushing yards.

For a running back, 5,000 yards is a career. For a quarterback, it puts you on a very short list. And on a night built around the run game, it’s fitting that both of them got there together.

And now, great news number three, and the biggest question from the start: how did Jim Leonhard beat Brian Flores at his own game?

Here are the numbers.

Flores blitzed on nearly seven out of every ten Josh Allen dropbacks.

Leonhard blitzed on just 11.5 percent of Minnesota’s dropbacks.

And Buffalo still generated more pressure: 42.3 percent to Minnesota’s 37.1 percent.

Pressure means the quarterback gets hurried, hit, or sacked. Flores needed extra rushers to get there. Leonhard didn’t.

And here’s why that’s so dangerous for opponents. When you blitz, you take players out of coverage to chase the quarterback. If the blitz doesn’t get there, someone is open. But when you can get pressure with just four rushers, you keep seven players in coverage. The quarterback is under fire, and there’s nowhere to throw the ball.

That’s what Leonhard did all night.

And think about how different this was from the week before. Against Baltimore, Leonhard blitzed on 64.5 percent of dropbacks, the team’s highest rate since 2021. Against Minnesota, he barely blitzed at all. Two completely different game plans, two dominant results. That’s a coordinator who can beat you any way he needs to.

Yardbarker said Leonhard called a fantastic game. Yahoo’s report card gave the defense top marks and credited Leonhard.

And the numbers were stunning.

The Vikings didn’t convert a single third down. Not one. All night.

Third down is usually a team’s last real chance to keep a drive alive. When you go zero for the night, every drive dies. And that’s how you end up with just six points in your own stadium.

Brady even won a challenge that gave Greg Rousseau a fumble recovery. He threw the red flag, asked the officials to take another look, and won.

Then Dorian Williams intercepted Carson Wentz. And on the very next series, Terrel Bernard picked him off again. And the home crowd in Minnesota started booing.

Christian Benford broke up a pass intended for Justin Jefferson, the receiver Brady coached at LSU and called the best in football. Jefferson spent part of the night looking frustrated on the bench.

And here’s how bad it got for Minnesota’s quarterback. Wentz finished with a passer rating of 8.9. That’s the lowest by any quarterback with at least 25 pass attempts since 2019. ESPN counted nine off-target throws. And ESPN had Wentz at a 2.3 QBR, on a scale of zero to 100, the lowest of any quarterback this season.

After the game, Kevin O’Connell passed on questions about trading for a quarterback and referred them to his general manager. Vikings fans booed. Wentz was eventually benched, and by the end of the night, he’d become a meme on social media. With the trade deadline the next day, Pro Football Talk said the Vikings needed to trade for a quarterback immediately, and Purple Insider said the Vikings were out of answers. O’Connell himself said his team has to improve on offense at a rapid pace.

Bills Mafia, remember when Joe Brady said Jim Leonhard and his staff are used to adjusting to whatever quarterback they face? This is what he meant.

So what does Monday night tell us about this Bills team?

First, Leonhard’s defense doesn’t need to blitz to take over a game. It can rush four, cover everyone else, and still be the scariest unit on the field. For every quarterback left on Buffalo’s schedule, that’s a problem with no easy answer.

Second, it tells you a lot about Joe Brady. Plenty of coaches would have panicked with two backups on the left side of the line against the most blitz-heavy defense in football. Brady didn’t. He trusted his line, leaned on Cook, and played the long game. That’s the kind of plan that wins in January.

And remember what he told the team before this game: don’t make it bigger than it is. Just play the next play. That’s exactly what they did.

And the kicker was perfect too. Tyler Bass hit field goals from 39, 25, and 52 yards. He’s now 19 for 19 on field goals this season, one of the most reliable kickers in the NFL.

Joe Brady has now won his first four road games as an NFL head coach, in his very first season. That’s four straight wins for Buffalo. And the Bills are seven and one for the first time since 1993.

Josh Allen spent the end of the night handing out souvenirs to fans.

And here’s the scary part for the rest of the league. Buffalo did all of this without Dion Dawkins and Alec Anderson. When those two get healthy, this line only gets stronger. And if they need more time, Monday night just proved the Bills have a Plan B that works against the toughest test in football.

And there’s even more good news since then. A day after the game, on trade deadline day, Brandon Beane traded a second-round pick to Tennessee for safety Amani Hooker, a player Beane compared to Micah Hyde. And Dion Dawkins restructured his contract at a community event to help make it happen. So this team just got stronger at exactly the right time.

So let’s put it all together.

Great news number one: Tylan Grable and Austin Corbett held up at left tackle and left guard against the most blitz-heavy defense Next Gen Stats has ever tracked.

Great news number two: Josh Allen and James Cook both passed 5,000 career rushing yards, the first teammates in NFL history to do it. Cook carried 31 times, the most by any player in any game this season, and scored a touchdown for the seventh straight game.

Great news number three: Jim Leonhard blitzed just 11.5 percent of the time and still generated more pressure than Minnesota. The Vikings went zero for the night on third down, scored just six points, and Carson Wentz finished with a passer rating of 8.9.

And the result: a 23 to 6 win, four straight victories, and a seven and one record for the first time since 1993.
